Startup funding: loan vs equity

A loan and an equity investment can both bring money into a business, but they work differently. The right choice depends on cash flow, growth plans, risk, terms and the legal rules that apply. This overview is educational, not personalised financial or legal advice.

How a loan generally works

A loan is money borrowed under agreed terms. It usually requires repayment according to a schedule, and may include interest, fees, security or personal guarantees. Repayments can place pressure on a business that does not yet generate reliable cash flow. Read the full terms and understand what happens if you miss a payment.

How equity generally works

Equity funding generally means an investor receives an ownership interest in the company. The business may not have the same scheduled repayment obligation as a conventional loan, but founders share ownership and may give up some control or a share of future value. Terms can include voting rights, information rights, preferences and other provisions.

Questions to ask about a loan

What is the total cost of borrowing? What are the repayment dates? Is collateral or a personal guarantee required? Are there fees or early repayment charges? Can the business reasonably make repayments under a conservative cash-flow scenario? What happens if revenue is delayed?

Questions to ask about equity

What percentage or instrument is being offered? How is the company valued? What rights does the investor receive? How could future fundraising dilute ownership? Are there governance or exit provisions? Have the documents been reviewed by a qualified professional?

Other possible sources

Depending on eligibility and business stage, founders may also consider grants, customer pre-orders, revenue-based arrangements, accelerators or strategic partnerships. These options are not automatically cheaper or safer; review their terms, restrictions and practical fit.

Do not choose based on the label alone

A loan is not always safer because ownership is retained, and equity is not always better because repayments may be absent. Compare the complete terms, downside risks and impact on the business. If the business is early and cash flow is uncertain, be especially careful about fixed repayment commitments.

Before signing

Ask an appropriately qualified legal, tax or financial professional to review the specific arrangement and local requirements. Do not promise guaranteed returns or accept money on terms you do not understand.
